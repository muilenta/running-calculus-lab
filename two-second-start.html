START WITH THE VELOCITY LINE

Three roads from velocity to position.

The line form is v(t) = mt + b, where m is acceleration and b is starting velocity. Here m = 2 and b = 0. To get position, account for all the motion over time, not just the final speed.

01 / LINE + AVERAGE VELOCITY

No integral notation needed.

Because velocity changes linearly, its average over 0 to t is exactly the average of its endpoints.

Average v = [v(0) + v(t)]/2
= (0 + 2t)/2 = t

x(t) = average v × t
= t2

At 2.5 s: average speed is (0 + 5)/2 = 2.5 m/s. Position change is 2.5 × 2.5 = 6.25 m.

For any straight velocity line v = mt + b: x(t) = x(0) + bt + ½mt2. Endpoint averaging is not generally exact for curved velocity graphs.

02 / INTEGRATION

Find the exact area.

Integrate velocity over elapsed time. The power rule gives the same position equation.

x(t) − x(0)
= ∫0t 2s ds
= [s2]0t
= t2

The variable s is just the time variable inside this integral. The endpoint t is the selected elapsed time.

At 2.5 s: 2.52 = 6.25 m.

03 / SUMMATION

Add little pieces of distance.

Split 0 to t into n equal slices, each with width Δt = t/n. In each slice, multiply a sampled velocity by that time width.

Δxi ≈ v(ti*) Δt
x(t) ≈ ∑i=1n v(ti*) Δt

As the largest slice width approaches zero, the sum approaches the integral.

x(t) = limn→∞ ∑i=1n v(ti*) Δt
= t2

The star means a chosen sample time within the slice. Try left edges, right edges, or midpoints below.

THE WORKED MATH / NO TRIGONOMETRY

How do you get from one graph to the next?

DIFFERENTIATION / FIND THE RATE

Bring down the power. Subtract one.

d(tn)/dt = ntn−1

01

Position → velocity

d(t2)/dt
= 2t2−1
= 2t = v(t)

The slope of the position curve tells you how fast the runner is moving.

02

Velocity → acceleration

d(2t)/dt
= 2 × 1
= 2 = a(t)

The velocity line has the same slope everywhere: speed increases by 2 m/s each second.

INTEGRATION / ADD UP THE CHANGE

Add one to the power. Divide by it.

∫tn dt = tn+1/(n + 1) + C   (n ≠ −1)

01

Acceleration → velocity

∫2 dt
= 2t + C1
= 2t = v(t)

Starting at rest means v(0) = 0, so C1 = 0.

02

Velocity → position

∫2t dt
= (2/2)t2 + C2
= t2 = x(t)

Starting at position zero means x(0) = 0, so C2 = 0.

YOUR TURN / 30 QUESTIONS TO EXPLORE

Think first. Then reveal the answer.

Unless a question changes the setup, use x(t) = t2, v(t) = 2t, and a(t) = 2, starting at rest at position zero. Time is in seconds. Click a question to open its worked answer; click again to hide it. Use the charts above to check your thinking.

01 / READ THE RUN

Where is he? How fast is he going?

1. What is the position after 1 second?

x(1) = 12 = 1 meter from the start.

2. What is the position after 2 seconds?

x(2) = 22 = 4 meters. Doubling the time from 1 to 2 seconds quadruples the position, because time is squared.

3. What is the velocity after 1 second?

v(1) = 2 × 1 = 2 m/s. Position and velocity have different units: at this instant he is 1 m from the start and moving at 2 m/s.

4. What is the velocity after 2 seconds?

v(2) = 2 × 2 = 4 m/s. That is about 6:42 per mile. The position is also numerically 4 here, but 4 m and 4 m/s mean different things.

5. What are position and velocity at the 2.5-second finish?

x(2.5) = 2.52 = 6.25 m; v(2.5) = 2 × 2.5 = 5 m/s, about 5:22 per mile. He is still moving when this example ends.

6. How far does he travel between 1 and 2 seconds?

Subtract positions: x(2) − x(1) = 4 − 1 = 3 meters. Since he only runs forward, distance and position change are equal.

02 / SLOPES AND THE POWER RULE

Read the rate of change.

7. How do you get velocity from x(t) = t²?

Differentiate with the power rule: d(t2)/dt = 2t2−1 = 2t = v(t). The position curve's tangent slope is velocity.

8. How do you get acceleration from v(t) = 2t?

d(2t)/dt = 2 × 1 = 2 m/s2. The derivative is constant, so the velocity graph is a straight line.

9. What is the position tangent's slope at 1.5 seconds?

The slope is v(1.5) = 2 × 1.5 = 3 m/s. At this point x(1.5) = 2.25 m; the tangent line is x = 2.25 + 3(t − 1.5), or x = 3t − 2.25.

10. What is the slope of the velocity line?

Use any two points, such as (0, 0) and (2.5, 5): slope = (5 − 0)/(2.5 − 0) = 2 m/s2. This is acceleration, not position.

11. Is average velocity over 0–2 seconds the same as velocity at 2 seconds?

No. Average velocity = (x(2) − x(0))/(2 − 0) = 4/2 = 2 m/s, the position graph's secant slope. Instantaneous velocity at 2 s is 4 m/s, the tangent slope there.

12. At the start, velocity is zero. Must acceleration also be zero?

No. Here v(0) = 0 but a(0) = 2 m/s2. Being at rest describes velocity at that instant; acceleration describes how velocity is changing as he starts.

03 / AREAS AND INTEGRATION

Build the accumulated change.

13. Integrate a(t) = 2 to find velocity. What happens to C?

∫2 dt = 2t + C. Starting at rest gives 0 = 2 × 0 + C, so C = 0 and v(t) = 2t. Starting velocity determines the integration constant.

14. Integrate v(t) = 2t to find position.

∫2t dt = (2/2)t2 + C = t2 + C. Since x(0) = 0, C = 0 and x(t) = t2.

15. What is the area under velocity from 0 to 2 seconds?

It is a triangle: ½ × 2 s × 4 m/s = 4 m. Integration agrees: ∫02 2t dt = [t2]02 = 4. This area gives position change.

16. What is the area under acceleration from 0 to 2 seconds?

It is a rectangle: 2 s × 2 m/s2 = 4 m/s. It gives velocity change, not distance. Add the starting velocity, zero, to get v(2) = 4 m/s.

17. Find the distance from 1 to 2 seconds using the velocity line, without integrating.

The area is a trapezoid. Average velocity = (v(1) + v(2))/2 = (2 + 4)/2 = 3 m/s. Multiply by 1 second: 3 meters. Endpoint averaging is exact because velocity is linear.

18. Why can't you just multiply the final 4 m/s by 2 seconds?

That gives 8 m and incorrectly assumes he ran at 4 m/s for the entire interval. He started at zero and sped up. Use the average, (0 + 4)/2 = 2 m/s: 2 × 2 = 4 m.

04 / BUILD A SUM

Turn rectangles into an integral.

19. Use four left-edge rectangles over 0–2 seconds. What is the sum?

Δt = 2/4 = 0.5 s. Sample at 0, 0.5, 1, and 1.5 s; velocities are 0, 1, 2, and 3 m/s.

(0 + 1 + 2 + 3) × 0.5 = 3 m, an underestimate of the exact 4 m.

20. Now use four right-edge rectangles. What changes?

Sample at 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 s; velocities are 1, 2, 3, and 4 m/s.

(1 + 2 + 3 + 4) × 0.5 = 5 m, an overestimate because velocity is increasing.

21. What do four midpoint rectangles give?

Sample at 0.25, 0.75, 1.25, and 1.75 s; velocities are 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, and 3.5 m/s.

(0.5 + 1.5 + 2.5 + 3.5) × 0.5 = 4 m, exactly. Midpoints are exact for this straight velocity line, but not generally for curved graphs.

22. What if you double to eight left- or right-edge rectangles over 0–2 seconds?

Each width is 0.25 s. Left-edge velocities are 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, and 3.5; their sum times 0.25 is 3.5 m. Right-edge velocities are 0.5 through 4 in steps of 0.5; the result is 4.5 m. Both are closer to the exact 4 m.

23. Can you write the right-edge sum and take its limit?

For 0–2 s, Δt = 2/n and ti = iΔt. Since v(ti) = 2iΔt:

Sn = ∑i=1n 2i(Δt)2
= (8/n2) ∑i=1n i
= (8/n2) × n(n + 1)/2
= 4(1 + 1/n) meters

Using ∑i=1n i = n(n + 1)/2, the limit as n grows without bound is 4 m, equal to ∫02 2t dt.

24. Why does velocity × slice width give meters, not meters per second?

The time units cancel: (m/s) × s = m. Adding these pieces accumulates position change. Acceleration × time instead gives (m/s2) × s = m/s, a velocity change.

05 / WHAT IF?

Change the setup. Rebuild the equations.

25. What if acceleration were 3 m/s² instead of 2?

Still starting at rest at position zero: a(t) = 3. Integrate once: v(t) = 3t. Integrate again: x(t) = 1.5t2. The position coefficient is half the acceleration.

This is a hypothetical variation; the charts above still use acceleration 2 m/s2.

26. With acceleration 3, what are position and velocity after 1 and 2 seconds?

At 1 s: x(1) = 1.5 × 12 = 1.5 m, and v(1) = 3 m/s.

At 2 s: x(2) = 1.5 × 22 = 6 m, and v(2) = 6 m/s. That second speed exceeds the lesson's 5:00-mile pace limit, so this variation would need to end sooner.

27. With acceleration 3, when would he reach the same 5 m/s target, and how far would he go?

Solve 3t = 5: t = 5/3 s ≈ 1.67 s. Then x = 1.5(5/3)2 = 25/6 m ≈ 4.17 m. Higher acceleration reaches the same speed sooner, over less distance. The final pace is still about 5:22 per mile.

28. Could acceleration 3 run for the same 2.5 seconds and still respect the pace limit?

No. v(2.5) = 3 × 2.5 = 7.5 m/s, about 3:35 per mile, faster than 5:00. An acceleration equation does not automatically cap speed. End the acceleration earlier; reaching the teaching target of 5 m/s takes 5/3 seconds.

29. Back to acceleration 2: what if he started already moving at 1 m/s?

Starting at position zero, integration gives v(t) = 1 + 2t and x(t) = t + t2. The initial velocity adds a constant to velocity and a linear term to position. These changed equations would also need a shorter interval to preserve the pace limit.

30. What if he started at position 10 m, still at rest with acceleration 2?

x(t) = 10 + t2; v(t) = 2t and a(t) = 2 are unchanged. At 2 s his position is 14 m, but he has traveled only 14 − 10 = 4 m. A different starting position shifts the position graph without changing its slopes.

t is in seconds; x in meters; v in m/s; a in m/s2. This example ends after 2.5 seconds, with the runner still moving.
